**`range()`** produces a sequence of numbers for loops. It is memory-efficient and very common.

## range(stop)

Numbers from `0` up to (but not including) `stop`:

<PythonExample
  filename="range_stop.py"
  code={`for i in range(5):
    print(i)`}
/>

## range(start, stop)

<PythonExample
  filename="range_start.py"
  code={`for i in range(2, 6):
    print(i)`}
/>

## range(start, stop, step)

<PythonExample
  filename="range_step.py"
  code={`for i in range(0, 10, 2):
    print(i)

for i in range(5, 0, -1):
print(i)`}
/>

## Convert to a list

`range` is not a list, but you can wrap it:

<PythonExample
  filename="list_range.py"
  code={`nums = list(range(1, 6))
print(nums)`}
/>

## Common patterns

<PythonExample
  filename="patterns.py"
  code={`# Print 10 times
for _ in range(10):
    print("Hello")

# Indexes 0..len-1

items = ["a", "b", "c"]
for i in range(len(items)):
print(i, items[i])`}
/>

<Callout variant="note">
  `_` as a variable name means “I do not need this value” — common in `for _ in
  range(n):`.
</Callout>

<Callout variant="tip">
  Prefer `for item in items:` over `for i in range(len(items)):` unless you need the index
  for something else.
</Callout>
